Here's the process. Six steps: context, problem, plan, build, test, review.

None of these are new. Good teams did all six long before AI. What changed is who does the work inside each step. An agent can now research, ask the questions, draft the plan, write the code, and even review code. What it can't do is decide what matters to your customer, or answer for it when a deploy goes wrong.

So for each step I'll show you two things: what the agent did, and what stayed with me. And notice that three of the six happen before anyone writes code.

Step one: context. Every session, an agent starts out knowing nothing about your business. When it doesn't know, it guesses, and its guesses sound just as confident as its facts.

So VideoArchiver has two plain text files that every agent reads before it does anything. The first, CONTEXT.md, is a glossary of my product's words: Destination, Storage preset, Failed clip, Failure episode, Replay. Each one is written down once, so "failed clip" means the same thing to me, to the agent, and in the code. The second, AGENTS.md, holds the rules for any agent working in the code.

In vertical software, those words are where your expertise lives. At Portico, the reports I build calculate missed time, makeup hours and expected hours for career schools. Anyone new to that code, person or agent, has to learn what those words mean to a school before their report can be right. So write the words down first. It's the cheapest step in this whole talk.

Step two: the problem. The usual way is to write a spec and hand it over. I do it the other way around: the agent interviews me.

Here's a real one, from this October. I wanted VideoArchiver customers to send their clips to storage they own beyond Google Drive and Dropbox: Backblaze, Cloudflare, Synology, even a NAS, a storage box at home. And I wanted more than one storage destination per camera link.

Before any interview, an agent researched it. It came back with a report and a demand table: 35 confirmed requests on Ring's community forum, 11 of them for a NAS. So I knew customers wanted it before I spent a day building it.

Then an agent grilled me. It worked through the decisions the feature needed, and every question came with its own recommended answer. That matters. I wasn't writing a spec from a blank page. I was saying yes or no to a proposal, and changing it where I knew better. 27 decisions, all settled in one session. Here's what that looked like.

This is a replay of that session. Three of the 27.

[→] The first decision: can one camera link send clips to several S3 destinations, the Backblaze and Cloudflare kind? The agent recommended one. I said several. Out of 27, that was the only time I went against its recommendation. It was a product call, and product calls are mine.

[→] The second one is technical, so here it is in plain words. If Ring's file server tells us how big the clip is, use that. If it doesn't, hold the clip in memory up to a limit, and never write it to disk. Above the limit, send it in pieces, and if the storage refuses, fail with a clear message. I said yes. Notice it kept a promise my product makes on its homepage: clips are streamed, never stored.

[→] Third: when a customer connects their storage, how do we know it works? Upload a tiny test file through the same path a real clip takes, then try to delete it. Yes. The customer finds out about a problem on the setup screen, instead of weeks later when a clip is missing.

[→] 27 decisions, one session, one override. The agent did the homework. I made the calls.

Step three: the plan. Still no code.

After the interview, I reviewed the architecture together with agents, and a second round of questions added 30 more decisions. Two of them show why this step matters. First, three parts of the code needed deeper work before anything new was built on top of them. Second, the database change ships in two steps, so no customer's save fails while the deploy is running. Neither of those is something I'd want to discover halfway through building. Both were caught on paper, when changing course costs nothing.

Then it became seven dated tracks, T0 through T6, each with its tickets, plus decision records: short notes that say what we chose and why. All of it drafted before any code was written.

VideoArchiver has 21 written decision records today. They're how the next agent, or the next engineer, finds out why things are the way they are, without asking me.

Small steps. Test first. A branch of its own.

Step four: build. This is what everyone pictures when they hear "AI writes code," and by this point it's the least dramatic part.

Each track gets a branch of its own, away from what customers use. The agent works in small steps: it writes a test first, watches it fail, then writes the code that makes it pass. Small steps also mean every change is small enough for a person to read, and that matters in step six.

Nobody needs to be a hero here. The process is boring on purpose, and that's how VideoArchiver gets built.

Step five: test. I treat a test as a gate. If it fails, nothing ships.

VideoArchiver has 390 test files, and they run on every pull request and every push to the main branch. The dashboard even has a demo mode with made-up cameras and clips, and the build checks that every page has one.

But the tests that matter most touch the real world. In vertical software, the real world is your customers' systems: their storage, their files, their edge cases. Here's the gate for the storage work. Before any storage preset ships, a small throwaway program streams 30 and 90 megabyte files to real Backblaze, Cloudflare and Synology buckets, plus two servers that people run themselves.

When I started writing this talk, that test hadn't run yet, and until it passed, those presets weren't going anywhere. It has run now, and it passed. That's what a gate is for. An agent will tell you the code works. The gate is how you find out.

A second agent reviews. A person reads every diff.

“Your team reviews everything I write before it ships.”

Step six: review. Before each track merges, a second agent reviews the code. It didn't write it, so it reads it fresh. Then I read the diff, the exact lines that changed. Then I deploy it, and I apply the database changes myself. No agent does those last steps.

Each release also gets a written record: what was checked before the deploy, each step in order, and what was verified on the live product afterwards. Both October releases have one, and the second fixed 17 issues found in an audit.

The rule holds on client work too. For two years I've been the embedded engineer on Portico's Success team, and their engineers review and QA everything before it ships. On my site I put it in one line: your team reviews everything I write before it ships. However the code got written, that line doesn't change.

What stays with a person

  • Choosing the problem
  • Picking the first failing test
  • Making the calls
  • Reading the diff
  • Reading the plan
  • Deploying

So here's what stays human, from everything you've just seen.

Deciding which problem is worth solving. Making the calls, including overriding the agent when it's wrong for your customer. Reading the plan before any code exists. Picking which test has to fail first, because that's how you define done. Reading the diff. And deploying, and changing the database, because when that goes wrong a customer loses something, and the agent isn't the one who calls them.

Notice that none of these take long. Reading a plan takes minutes, and so does reading a small diff. Everything else I hand over gladly: the research, the questions, the drafts, the code, a first review. That's most of the hours.

For your next AI-built feature:

  1. Who wrote down the problem?
  2. Who read the plan?
  3. Which test fails first?
  4. Who reviewed the diff?

Which brings me to my question for Monday.

Pick your next feature that AI is helping build, and ask four things. Who wrote down the problem? Who read the plan? Which test fails first? And who reviewed the diff?

If you're a founder or an operator, ask your team. If you're an investor, ask the companies you back. If every answer comes back with a name, or a test, you're in control. If any answer is "the AI handled it," that's the step to fix first. You don't need new tools for that. You need a person next to each step. Take a photo of this slide, and ask on Monday.
